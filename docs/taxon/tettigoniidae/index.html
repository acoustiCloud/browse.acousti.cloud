diagnosis · Plazi

Diagnosis Distinguished by small size of adults, macropterous males, apterous females. In addition, the stridulatory file of males is positioned on a raised swelling (e.g. Figs 20A, B ). The stridulatory file is short and the number of teeth is low.

discussion · Plazi

Comments. This subfamily currently comprises 49 tribes, 384 genera, and nearly 3000 valid species worldwide, with the greatest diversity occurring in tropical regions ( Cigliano et al. 2025 ). Members of the Phaneropterinae , commonly known as bush katydids, range from tiny species, such as those in the tribes Phaneropterini or Pycnopalpini , to large-bodied forms found in tribes like Steirodontini or Vossiini ( Bey-Bienko 1954 , Nickle 1992 , Cohn et al. 2014 , Cadena-Castañeda 2014a , 2015a , d, 2016 ). They are characterized by a generally globose head, although some tribes, such as Dysoniini and Aegimiini , exhibit a pronounced vertex ( Nickle 1992 , Cadena-Castañeda 2011 , Dias et al. 2012 ). The lateral lobes of the pronotum almost entirely cover the auditory spiracle, which is elliptical. The first tarsomere is cylindrical and not laterally sulcate; the prosternum is unarmed, and the tympanum is generally exposed on both sides of the fore tibia, although in some taxa it may be covered by cuticle, leaving only a slit open through which sound can reach the tympanic organ. The female ovipositor varies greatly, adapting to the oviposition requirements of each species, so no single ovipositor morphology characterizes the subfamily ( Brunner von Wattenwyl 1878 , Bey-Bienko 1954 , Ragge 1980 , Nickle 1992 ). Phaneropterine eggs are typically flattened and ovoid (although in some rare cases they may be asymmetrical), which serves as a diagnostic trait for the subfamily ( Heller & Hemp 2024 ). Most species are green, but yellowish, brown, pink (usually due to erythrism), white, and black individuals may also occur. It is common for species to blend into vegetation, while others resemble lichens and bryophytes, or in some cases, mimic ants in their nymphal stages and wasps in adulthood ( Brunner von Wattenwyl 1878 , Leroy 1985 , Nickle 1992 , Cadena-Castañeda et al. 2022 ). Phaneropterines are unique among Tettigoniidae in that females produce response signals to the conspecific male calling song. To produce these sounds, females possess a structure analogous to the male stridulatory file, consisting of a complex of rigid, recurved spines on the anal area of the tegmina ( Nickle & Carlysle 1975 ). Males respond to the female reply by actively moving toward the sound source, rather than calling from a fixed location ( Heller & Helversen 1986 , Spooner 1995 ). Male calls are diverse and involve remarkable acoustic adaptations in the stridulatory apparatus ( Heller & Hemp 2014 ). Some species produce among the most complex songs known in insects (Nickle 1984, Walker 2004 ). In many cases, species are morphologically very similar, making acoustic signals an essential tool for species identification ( Leroy 1970 , Walker et al. 2003 , Heller et al. 2015 ). As evidenced above, the Phaneropterinae are highly diverse in multiple aspects and are clearly distinguishable from other subfamilies of Tettigoniidae . However, due to the high number of taxa, the systematics and delimitation of this group remain in progress. This contribution focuses on the classification and delineation of short-winged tribes and other related groups, to resolve longstanding classification challenges and clarify the identity of traditional and derivative taxa.
